You are warmly invited to spend a little time with a simple question:

**What’s a better thought?**

It is not a complicated question, and it does not demand a perfect answer. It is simply a gentle way of turning our attention toward thoughts that bring a little more peace, hope, kindness, or encouragement.

As you read these pages, you may find yourself remembering good moments, noticing new possibilities, or discovering thoughts that help lighten your heart.

If that happens—even a little—then this book will have done what I hoped it might do.

## **How to Use This Book**

This book is not meant to be rushed through or studied like a textbook. It is meant to be a gentle companion.

You may read it from beginning to end, or simply open to any page that catches your interest. Each chapter offers ideas, reflections, and questions that may help you notice your thoughts and discover ones that feel kinder, wiser, or more hopeful.

You might pause sometimes and ask yourself the small question that inspired this book:

**What’s a better thought?**

There is no single right answer. The better thought is simply the one that helps you feel a little more peaceful, a little more encouraged, or a little more able to enjoy the day.

Take your time. Reflect if you wish. Smile when something rings true. And return to the question whenever you need it.

## **Introduction**

My intention in writing this book is to share an idea that helped me move away from sadness, depression, and grief, and return to enjoying life again—with hope and appreciation for the good memories that are always there.

One of the most surprising discoveries of my life was realizing how much my thoughts were shaping the way I felt and what I did.

When I talk about *a better thought*, I’m talking about gently moving away from worry, sadness, rumination, anxiety, anger, and self-criticism—and choosing thoughts that help me feel calmer, happier, and more at peace.

**What would you rather be thinking?**

We all have moments when we suddenly notice:

*(Uh oh, my mind is not being very kind to me right now. It happens. Minds can be a bit dramatic.)*

That’s the moment when this question becomes powerful:

**What’s a better thought?**

That small question became a gentle guide, helping me turn my attention toward thoughts that brought comfort, strength, and even moments of happiness.

In this book, I explore the kinds of thoughts that can lift us up, the skills that help us shift our thinking, the reasons why better thoughts matter, and the \\obstacles that sometimes get in the way.

My hope is that this little question—*What’s a better thought?*—might become a helpful companion for you as well.

# **Chapter One**

## **What’s a Better Thought?**

It happened in the middle of a Marriage Enrichment retreat.

There were about twenty couples there, divided into small groups of five couples each. Jim and I had been involved in Marriage Enrichment for forty years. We had taught it, led it, believed in it, and—most importantly—practiced it ourselves.

We didn’t just tell other couples to listen to each other. We listened to each other. We didn’t just teach communication exercises. We did them. Year after year, we worked on our own marriage along with the couples we were helping.

By then, after almost sixty years together, I thought our marriage was about as wonderful as a marriage could possibly be.

Well, almost.

There was still Jim’s driving.

I never did learn to sit peacefully beside him. When I thought he was getting too close to something, I would gasp or stomp my foot on an imaginary brake pedal, and Jim would get irritated with me. After forty years of Marriage Enrichment, we had apparently solved everything except how Jim drove and how I reacted to it.

But otherwise, we had built something extraordinary together.

And now I was beginning to be afraid I was going to lose him.

### **What If This Is Our Last Christmas?**

It had begun in November 2015\.

Jim was seventy-nine and becoming unusually tired. At first we didn’t know what was wrong. Then the lymph nodes under his chin and along the side of his head began swelling. He seemed to be swelling up before my eyes.

He went to the doctor. Maybe it was mononucleosis, they thought. Could it be mumps? But Jim kept getting worse.

Christmas was approaching, and we had planned to drive from San Jose to our son’s home in San Diego. Finally we told the family we didn’t think we could make the trip. Dad wasn’t feeling well enough, and the drive would be too much for him.

So they came to us instead.

I remember looking around at my family that Christmas and having a frightening thought:

**What if this is our last Christmas?**

I didn’t know that it was.

But some part of me was already afraid.

By January, Jim still had not been diagnosed. We were the president couple of the Silicon Valley chapter of Better Marriages, and our annual retreat was coming up. Because Jim felt so poorly, we had already arranged for another couple to take over. We would attend, but we wouldn’t lead one of the small groups.

Then, the day before the retreat, the new leader called us in tears. Another leadership couple had become ill and couldn’t come.

She hated to ask us. She knew how Jim was feeling. But did we possibly want to take one of the groups?

Jim and I were both on the phone.

“We’ll do it,” he said.

So we did.

It took an enormous effort for him just to be there.

During one of our small-group sessions, a couple began talking about Christmas. Their daughter was getting married, and they realized this had been their last Christmas with their family exactly as it had always been.

Their last Christmas.

Something inside me broke.

I began crying quietly.

When it was time for another couple to have a dialogue, Jim looked at me with concern and said maybe we needed a turn. 

And in front of that little circle of people, I told my husband what I had been terrified to say.

“I can’t live without you.”

I cried and cried.

I told him I didn’t know what I would do if whatever was happening to him turned out to be serious.

Jim did what we had taught other people to do for decades.

He listened.

He didn’t tell me not to be ridiculous. He didn’t try to talk me out of being frightened. He active-listened to me and let me say what I was feeling.

The group listened too. They were wonderfully warm and caring.

Strangely, saying the horrible thing helped.

I had gotten it out of me. I had said aloud what I was most afraid of. And I think that helped me become more able to support Jim through what was coming.

I had no idea how much support he was going to need.

### **Valiant and Scared**

In February, Jim was finally diagnosed with CLL, chronic lymphocytic leukemia.

Then one day, after a blood test, the doctor told him to go to the hospital immediately.

That began the final three and a half months of his life.

We were mostly alone together during those months. His treatments weakened his immune system, and it was winter and flu season. We were afraid for him to be around many people.

So our world became very small.

There were doctors and hospitals and treatments. There was a port for his medicine, and when he wanted a shower we had to wrap his arm carefully in Saran Wrap to keep the water away from it. Getting him into the shower, getting everything protected, getting him washed and safely out again could take half the day.

And somehow we laughed.

There were strawberry milkshakes after doctors’ appointments and treatments.

There were emails. I wrote to perhaps sixty people, telling them how Jim was doing. I tried to include something hopeful—some good day he had had, some encouraging possibility. And I asked people to send us jokes.

They did.

We needed those jokes.

We listened to music on our wonderful stereo. I would choose Beethoven. Jim might choose *Grand Canyon Suite*. We would listen and talk about what we loved in the music.

Sometimes I read him stories.

Once, when I took my car in for a smog check, I discovered that the man who ran the place had an immense, magnificent aquarium filled with marvelous fish.

I asked him, “Could I bring my husband here to see your fish?”

“Absolutely,” he said.

So one day I brought Jim. He could barely walk far enough to get inside, but we made it. We looked at those amazing fish and even got to feed them.

In the middle of leukemia, we had an adventure at the smog-check station.

I didn’t know it then, but I was already doing something I would spend years learning to do more deliberately.

I was looking for a better thought.

Or perhaps a better moment.

Not because the frightening things weren’t real. They were terribly real.

There were nights in the emergency room when Jim couldn’t stand or even sit any longer. Once, when there was no bed available, he stretched out on a hard bench with my coat underneath his head. I finally called Sandy and Russ and said, “Please. I need help.”

They came.

They didn’t fix anything. They were simply there with us.

When Jim was admitted to the hospital, I didn’t want to leave him. Sometimes they gave me a reclining chair, but I discovered that I slept better in my car. I would go out to the hospital parking lot, where a security guard walked around during the night, find a place where I felt safe, and sleep there.

For a while my diet seemed to consist largely of Ensure and PowerBars.

When things were a little brighter, I would go down to the hospital cafeteria and find little goodies for the two of us.

And there were always those strawberry milkshakes.

We were valiant and scared.

Courageous and petrified.

Both were true.

### **The Night Lynn Called**

My friend Lynn called me often.

Most people wrote emails, or I called them. But Lynn called.

Sometimes she seemed to call at precisely the moment I needed her most.

One night I was sitting in my car while the doctors were giving Jim a new treatment. They had run out of good choices. They were going to try something different, but they couldn’t tell us what would happen.

He might not survive the treatment.

He might die that night.

They had already told us to gather the family.

I sat in the car waiting and wondering whether my husband would be alive in the morning.

And Lynn called.

It was hard on her, too. Again and again she was the person who happened to call when another piece of terrible news had arrived.

But that night Jim didn’t die.

In fact, he rallied.

For a little while in April, he became well enough to do some wonderfully ordinary things.

He got a haircut.

He went to the bank.

He got a pizza and had his best friend over.

I even went out to lunch with a friend, one of the first times I had left him socially since he became ill.

For a couple of days, life almost looked like life again.

Then he went down.

And this time he didn’t come back.

### **When His Heart Stopped**

Jim died on a Thursday morning in the middle of May, at 9:40.

I was holding him.

He was unconscious. I had my arms wrapped around him and my head against his chest. I could hear his heart. I could hear him breathing.

His breathing became faster and faster.

So did his heart.

I kept talking to him.

I told him how much I loved him.

I told him how much I cared about him.

I told him what a wonderful man he had been and what a magnificent life I had had because of him.

And then I heard his heart stop.

I heard his last breath.

He was dead.

I was devastated.

And I was glad.

Glad for him.

The suffering was over.

That was one of the strangest things about those months and about grief afterward: completely opposite feelings could both be true.

I could be destroyed because Jim was dead and grateful that he no longer had to suffer.

I went home.

Our sons, Jim and Ken, came. Our daughter-in-law Gerrie came. The family gathered around me.

Mostly I stayed in bed.

Jim and I had talked about death before he became ill. He had told me that after he died, whatever I wanted was what he wanted.

“I won’t be here,” he had essentially said. “Do whatever makes you most comfortable.”

He thought cremation made sense, if that was all right with me.

I couldn’t bear the thought of a formal memorial service. I couldn’t imagine waiting and then going through the agony all over again.

So I did something I could bear.

I invited people to our house.

They could come Saturday evening for wine and snacks or Sunday morning for coffee and doughnuts.

And they came.

They met my sons and Gerrie and my grandchildren. We were together. We remembered Jim.

At one of those gatherings, several friends asked me, “What can we do for you?”

I knew the answer.

“Call me. Write to me.”

So Edna got organized.

Someone would call Sunday. Someone else Monday. Someone Tuesday and others the rest of the week

And they did it.

Faithfully.

Some of them kept calling for years.

### **Maybe It Was Jim**

Lynn had told me something else after Jim died.

“Watch,” she said. “See if he contacts you somehow.”

I didn’t particularly believe that.

But I looked anyway.

A couple of days after Jim died, I went swimming in our pool. The Polaris pool cleaner had gotten stuck because something had wrapped around one of its wheels, and I was trying to untangle it.

A bee appeared.

It hovered around me and around the machine.

And it stayed.

And stayed.

I wasn’t afraid of it.

Instead, I had the strangest, sweetest thought.

**Maybe that’s Jim.**

Maybe he was coming to tell me I was going to be all right.

And maybe, knowing Jim, he was doing it in a slightly funny way.

I even called Gerrie to come look because the bee’s behavior seemed so unusual.

A few days later, I opened my bedroom curtains in the morning and there was a bee right in the middle of the window screen.

It was still, just there.

When I came upstairs that afternoon for a nap, it was still there. This time it moved, cleaning its legs and face.

Then it flew away.

Was it Jim?

I don’t know.

I didn’t have to know.

**Maybe it was Jim,** was a much better thought than **Jim is completely gone forever.**

And for then, maybe was enough.

### **“The Best Is Yet to Be”**

A few weeks after Jim died, some of our Marriage Enrichment friends invited me to dinner at one of our favorite restaurants, the Cheesecake Factory.

I had already turned down one invitation to go out with a group.

Then I thought perhaps I should say yes to something.

So I went.

They were very sweet to me, and we actually had a lovely evening.

At one point we were talking about hope.

My friend Linda suddenly said, “And the best is yet to be.”

I think perhaps she surprised herself.

She certainly surprised me.

Everything inside me wanted to shout:

**NO\!**

How could the best possibly be yet to come?

The best had just died.

Nothing would ever be that good again.

Instead, I looked at her, probably looking agonized, and said something like:

“Oh…do you think?”

We hugged.

And that was all.

Except it wasn’t all.

The sentence came home with me.

**The best is yet to be.**

I didn’t believe it.

But a tiny question had entered my mind.

*Could there still be something good?*

Not best.

I couldn’t imagine best.

But something?

Could there possibly be a real life after this?

I didn’t know.

But I had begun to wonder.

### **They Were Watching Me**

My friends kept calling.

They took me to lunch and dinner. They asked how I was doing. They listened.

And gradually another thought occurred to me.

Many of these women were twenty or thirty years younger than I was. I had known some of them since their children were young, when I taught Parent Effectiveness Training. Others had known Jim and me through Marriage Enrichment.

And I began to wonder whether they were watching me for another reason.

Maybe they were afraid too.

Maybe somewhere inside themselves they were thinking, *Someday my husband could die. What would happen to me? Could I survive it?*

And now here I was.

A living demonstration.

I never asked them whether that was what they were thinking. I didn’t need to.

It was a good thought.

**Maybe if I survive this, I can show them that they could survive it too.**

I had spent much of my adult life teaching.

Perhaps this was something else I was being asked to teach.

But first I had to survive it.

And there was something I hadn’t told them.

For some time, I had been carrying another plan inside me.

Before Jim died, I had heard that sometimes when people had been married for many years and one of them died, the other died soon afterward.

I hoped that would happen to me.

It seemed comforting.

Perhaps I would simply die too.

And if I didn’t—if life without Jim became as unbearable as I expected it to be—I had decided that I could end my own life.

At the time, that thought gave me relief.

Dying seemed like a better thought than living without Jim.

But I was still here.

And these women were watching me live.

### **The Hours When Nobody Was There**

I got all the help I could.

I went to a grief group.

I had a therapist at Kaiser.

A friend who had worked as a grief counselor let me tell the horrible story—the illness, the treatments, the despair, the suffering.

I cried.

I talked.

I let people help me.

All of that mattered enormously.

But eventually lunch ended.

The friend went home.

The telephone call ended.

The television went off.

And then there was just me and my mind.

My mind had discovered that it could replay the last months of Jim’s life with astonishing clarity.

It could take me back to the hospital.

To the treatments.

To his suffering.

To the frightening nights.

To every moment when we had despaired.

Again and again and again.

And finally something occurred to me.

If my mind could take me back to the terrible things so vividly, couldn’t it take me somewhere else?

Couldn’t I choose another memory?

I found a scrap of paper.

And I wrote:

**What’s a better thought?**

Then I looked for one.

And there was Jim.

Not sick.

Not in a hospital bed.

Not dying.

Jim was coming down our stairs.

### **Jim in His Fedora**

After retirement, Jim volunteered as a docent at the Computer History Museum, where one of his jobs was demonstrating the IBM 1401 computer.

Our son Jim suggested that his father ought to demonstrate it in costume.

“What kind of costume?” my husband asked.

Our son explained.

A white shirt.

A tie.

A three-piece suit.

His lace-up shoes.

In other words, dress exactly as a businessman might have dressed when the 1401 was new in 1959\.

Jim thought this was a splendid idea.

So on Saturday mornings, he would get all dressed up to go demonstrate the computer.

I would be sitting on the couch, and when I saw him coming down the stairs, I would get up to admire him.

He looked gorgeous.

And I told him so.

Jim would become sheepish about being admired.

But he would let me do it.

Sometimes he would even button his jacket for me.

And then came the finishing touch.

His fedora.

With a little feather in it.

There he was.

My beautiful husband.

And for a moment, I wasn’t in the hospital.

I wasn’t listening to his heart stop.

I wasn’t imagining the rest of my life without him.

I was at the bottom of our stairs, admiring the man I loved.

And I smiled.

Nothing had changed.

Jim was still dead.

But **I had changed what I was experiencing for that moment.**

So I tried another memory.

Jim swimming and swimming and swimming in our pool, then stopping his laps to dive down and inspect the black algae, attacking it with his pumice stone.

Another.

One of our romantic evenings together: champagne and lobster Thermidor—and afterward a Band-Aid for Jim’s hand because the lobster had fought back.

I began collecting them.

Better thoughts.

Better memories.

Not because the terrible memories weren’t true.

They were.

But the beautiful memories were true too.

Why should the worst three and a half months of our nearly sixty years together be allowed to occupy my mind while all those thousands of wonderful days waited quietly somewhere else?

I had stumbled onto something.

I didn’t understand all of it yet.

I certainly didn’t know that ten years later I would still be practicing it, deliberately recalling a wonderful memory every morning—memories not only of Jim, but of my whole life: people I have loved, things I have accomplished, adventures, peak experiences, ridiculous moments, beautiful moments, times when I was brave, times when I was useful, times when I was simply happy.

Back then, I only knew that I was suffering and that sometimes I could change what was happening inside me by asking one small question.

I had thought the better thought might be dying.

Then other people began lending me thoughts.

*Maybe Jim is still near.*

*Maybe these women need to see that I can survive.*

*Maybe there is still something good ahead.*

And finally I began learning how to find them for myself.

I could not change the fact that Jim had died.

I could not think my way out of grief.

I could not make those terrible three and a half months unhappen.

But perhaps I didn’t have to let my mind live there all day.

I could choose, for this moment, to go somewhere else.

Back to the swimming pool.

Back to the music.

Back to the lobster Thermidor.

Back to the stairs.

There was Jim again, buttoning his jacket and putting on that wonderful fedora with the little feather because he knew I loved looking at him.

I could see him.

I could feel the love.

I could smile.

And then I could ask myself again:

**What’s a better thought?**

# **Chapter 2**

## **When the Mind Gets Stuck**

After Jim died, I discovered that my mind could get stuck.

It could pick one terrible scene from the last months of his life and play it again.

And again.

And again.

I didn't have to invite the memory in. It knew how to find me all by itself.

Eventually I began deliberately replacing some of those terrible scenes with other memories from our nearly sixty years together. That was the beginning of my question:

**What's a better thought?**

But grief didn't invent my tendency to get stuck in a thought.

I had been doing it all my life.

Perhaps you have too.

### **The Thought That Won't Go Away**

Sometimes something worries me and my mind seems to decide:

*This is important. We'd better keep thinking about it.*

Unfortunately, thinking about it for the 47th time doesn't necessarily accomplish anything that thinking about it the first 46 times failed to accomplish.

But my mind can be very persistent.

There is a word for some of this: **rumination**.

Researchers sometimes use the broader term **repetitive negative thinking** for negative thoughts that are intrusive, repetitive, and difficult to disengage from.

I think of it more simply.

**My mind is stuck.**

Something has captured my attention, and I keep circling it.

Sometimes the concern is legitimate.

Sometimes it is exaggerated.

Sometimes it is highly improbable.

And sometimes, when I finally get far enough away from it, I wonder:

*What in the world was I thinking?*

The difficulty is that while I am inside the thought, it can feel completely reasonable.

### **I Was Going to Be Kidnapped**

At one point in my life I became frightened that I might be kidnapped.

I don't remember everything that started it, but I remember that the possibility became very real in my mind.

I thought about it too much.

Finally I talked to my son.

He didn't give me a long lecture about anxiety. He didn't try to prove that nothing bad could ever happen to me.

He asked me a question.

Were people living in my retirement community being kidnapped?

Well.

No.

That was rather inconvenient for my kidnapping story.

His question made me step outside the story my mind had constructed and look at what was actually happening around me.

I was living among all these other people.

They weren't disappearing.

Nobody seemed particularly interested in kidnapping any of us.

Perhaps I had mistaken a **possible** event for a **probable** event.

Those are very different things.

And suddenly my frightening thought didn't seem quite so convincing.

My son had given me a better thought.

Or perhaps, more accurately, he had given me a **better question**.

### **Go Outside and Look at Your House**

Many years earlier, I had another fixation.

I became afraid our house was going to be robbed.

I worried enough about it that we eventually got a security system.

Then a friend gave me an interesting assignment.

“Go outside,” my friend said.

So I did.

“Look at your house. Then look at the other houses in the neighborhood. What is there about your house that would make somebody particularly want to rob yours?”

That changed something.

Inside my head, I had been starring in a story called:

**Our House Is Going to Be Robbed.**

Outside on the street, I was simply looking at a house among other houses.

My friend had moved me from **imagination to observation**.

That has become one of the ways I think about better thoughts now.

Sometimes I don't need to manufacture a happier thought.

I need to **look at the evidence**.

What do I actually know?

What am I assuming?

Is this happening—or am I imagining that it might happen?

How likely is it?

What would I tell a friend who had exactly this worry?

A better thought isn't necessarily a cheerful thought.

Sometimes a better thought is simply a **truer thought**.

### **The Day I Accidentally Blasted Myself Out of a Thought**

One of my favorite experiences with a stuck mind happened completely by accident.

I was driving somewhere and worrying.

I can't even remember what I was worrying about now, which tells you something about the importance of the worry.

At the time, however, I was going over and over it.

I knew I was doing it.

*Stop thinking about this,* I probably told myself.

That didn't work.

So I decided to change the music. Maybe different music would help change my mood.

I reached for the control.

And I hit the wrong thing.

Instead of changing the music, I turned the volume up enormously.

**BLAST\!**

The sound was terrific.

And suddenly—

**the thought was gone.**

Whatever I had been perseverating about had simply vanished from the center of my attention.

I had startled myself right out of it.

I remember being fascinated.

*Where did the thought go?*

The problem hadn't been solved.

I hadn't reasoned my way out of it.

Nobody had reassured me.

I hadn't found a brilliant piece of wisdom.

Something unexpected had simply interrupted the loop.

That experience stayed with me.

Apparently my attention was not as permanently attached to that thought as it had felt.

It could move.

### **Can a Brain Get Into a Rut?**

This makes me think of a wagon wheel traveling over the same dirt road again and again.

Eventually there are grooves, ruts.

Once the wheels fall into those grooves, that's the direction the wagon naturally wants to travel.

Our brains are vastly more complicated than a dirt road, of course. But I find the image useful.

Things we repeatedly think about can become familiar mental ruts or grooves.

Worry can become a rut.

Self-criticism can become a rut.

Replaying an argument can become a rut.

So can gratitude. A better groove. (*Groovy*\!)

So can remembering someone we love.

So can looking for goodness.

The fascinating part for me is that the brain isn't simply a container holding thoughts. It is an active physical organ made up of interconnected networks. Scientists are still learning how these networks contribute to attention, emotion, memory, depression, anxiety, and our ability to shift from one mental state to another.

And recently I learned about something that made me think about my stuck thoughts in a completely new way.

### **A Magnetic Nudge to the Brain**

It is called **transcranial magnetic stimulation**, or **TMS**.

TMS is a genuine medical treatment, not a metaphor. A clinician places an electromagnetic coil against the head and delivers carefully controlled magnetic pulses. Those pulses induce small electrical currents that stimulate neurons and neural circuits in targeted areas of the brain.

A repeated form called rTMS is used clinically for certain conditions, particularly depression that has not responded adequately to other treatment. Researchers are continuing to investigate exactly how changing activity in interconnected brain networks can improve mood.

I find this astonishing.

Here is a person whose brain has become caught in patterns associated with serious depression. And physicians have developed a way of applying magnetic stimulation to particular areas of the brain and, for some people, helping those patterns change.

Now, I want to be very clear.

**Turning up my car radio was not TMS.**

And asking *What's a better thought?* is not a substitute for medical treatment for depression or another serious mental illness.

But learning about TMS gave me a wonderful image.

**The brain is not necessarily stuck forever just because it feels stuck right now.**

Something can change.

Awareness can begin.

A circuit can behave differently.

Attention can move. 

The mind can find another track.

And for the ordinary stuck thoughts of everyday life, perhaps we have our own collection of small nudges available.

### **Interrupt the Pattern**

My deafening radio taught me something before I knew what to call it:

**Sometimes interrupting a thought is more useful than arguing with it.**

Change the music.

Stand up.

Walk outside.

Call someone.

Take a shower.

Look at something beautiful.

Say a prayer.

Read something funny.

Take ten slow breaths.

Go into another room.

Remember the man in the fedora.

None of these guarantees that the unwanted thought will disappear.

And sometimes a painful thought needs our attention. There may be a decision to make, a loss to grieve, a problem to solve, an apology to give, or help we need to seek.

But there is a difference between **thinking about a problem** and **circling a problem**.

One may lead somewhere.

The other keeps bringing me back to the same spot.

I'm trying to learn to notice the difference.

### **Is This Thought Helping Me?**

I've discovered that I can ask myself several questions from Byron Kady when my mind gets stuck:

**Is this thought true?**

**How do I know?**

**Is it probable, or merely possible?**

**Am I solving something—or just going around again?**

**Is there anything I can actually do about this right now?**

And finally:

**What's a better thought?**

Sometimes the answer is a happier thought.

Sometimes it's a more realistic thought.

Sometimes it's a funny thought.

Sometimes it is simply:

*I have thought about this enough for now.*

That can be a very good thought.

### **The Fly on the Masterpiece**

I once came across an image from Marc and Angel that stayed with me.

Imagine that an enormous, magnificent work of art is hanging in front of you—beautiful colors, wonderful textures, extraordinary detail.

And then a little dark fly lands on it.

Where do you look?

At the fly.

Of course you notice the fly. Our minds are very good at noticing what is wrong.

But why spend the next twenty minutes staring at it?

That's the part I'm interested in.

Life contains flies.

It contains disappointments, mistakes, frightening possibilities, irritating people, physical discomfort, embarrassing memories and red lights when we're already late.

I'm not advocating pretending that the fly isn't there.

I'm asking:

**Do I want to spend all afternoon looking at it?**

### **The Story I Am Telling Myself**

There is another piece of this that fascinates me.

We are storytellers.

Something happens, and almost immediately we begin explaining it to ourselves.

Someone doesn't answer my message.

*She's angry with me.*

I wake up feeling tired.

*This whole day is going to be awful.*

Someone I love sounds irritated.

*I've done something wrong.*

Something could conceivably happen.

*It's going to happen.*

Notice what has occurred.

A fact has become a story.

And often I am reacting emotionally not only to what happened, but to **the story I created about what happened**.

This gives imagination tremendous power.

We can use imagination to frighten ourselves.

I certainly have.

If I can create an entire kidnapping story without anyone actually kidnapping anybody, my imagination is working extremely well\!

So perhaps imagination can work in another direction too.

What else might be true?

What else could happen?

What story would I rather rehearse?

Not:

*Everything will turn out exactly as I want.*

I can't know that.

But perhaps:

*There are several ways this could turn out.*

Or:

*Maybe she is busy and hasn't seen my message.*

Or:

*I feel awful at seven in the morning surprisingly often, and I frequently feel quite good by ten.*

Or:

*I don't know what's going to happen yet.*

**I don't know yet** can be an excellent better thought.

It leaves the future open.

### **Borrow a Brain**

Sometimes I can't get myself out of my own thought.

That's when another person can be invaluable.

My son did it with kidnapping.

My friend did it with the house.

After Jim died, Linda did it when she said:

**“The best is yet to be.”**

I couldn't believe her.

But I could borrow the possibility.

Sometimes another person is standing outside the mental room we're trapped in and can see the door.

That's one reason conversation has mattered so much in my life.

We don't always need someone to tell us what to do.

Sometimes we need someone to listen long enough that we can hear ourselves.

Sometimes we need a question.

Sometimes we need another interpretation.

Sometimes we need someone to say:

*Have you considered this?*

I think of this as borrowing another brain for a few minutes.

Then I can come back to mine with more possibilities.

### **Better Does Not Mean Positive**

This distinction matters enormously to me.

I am **not** suggesting that whenever something terrible happens we should paste a happy thought over it.

Jim was dying.

“Everything is wonderful\!” would not have been a better thought.

It would have been nonsense.

When Jim died, I needed to grieve.

I needed to cry.

I needed people.

I needed a grief group and a therapist and friends who would let me tell the terrible stories.

Better thinking did not mean denying reality.

It meant discovering that reality was **larger than the worst thing in it**.

Jim suffered terribly during the final months of his life.

True.

Jim and I also had nearly sixty years together.

True.

I heard his heart stop.

True.

I also watched him come down the stairs hundreds of times and thought he was gorgeous.

True.

My mind had been selecting one part of reality and replaying it.

I learned that I could sometimes select another part.

**A better thought does not have to contradict the painful thought.**

It can sit beside it.

*This is terribly hard.*

And:

*I have people who love me.*

*I am frightened.*

And:

*I don't know yet what will happen.*

*I miss him terribly.*

And:

*What a magnificent life we had.*

Both can be true.

### **The Second Thought**

Perhaps we can't always control the first thought that appears.

I certainly can't.

My brain wakes up some mornings and apparently begins work before consulting me.

It finds a worry.

It remembers a failure.

It predicts something unpleasant.

Fine.

There it is.

But perhaps the first thought doesn't have to get the last word.

That's the skill I want to practice.

**Notice.**

*Oh. I'm doing it again.*

**Pause.**

*Is this helping me?*

**Check.**

*Is it true? Is it probable? Is there something I need to do?*

**Interrupt.**

*Move. Breathe. Listen. Look. Call someone. Change something.*

And then:

**Choose again.**

*What's a better thought?*

Not necessarily the perfect thought.

Not the happiest thought imaginable.

Just one that moves me a little closer to truth, peace, possibility, love, humor, gratitude, or action.

Maybe that's all getting unstuck really requires.

Not forcing the mind to leap from despair to ecstasy.

Just helping it move **one thought over**.

And then perhaps one thought more.

**Just in case…**

Lately I’ve been setting my watch for 16 minutes to become aware of what I’m thinking and look for love and goodness.

*I'm tired now.*

*I'm discouraged now.*

*I'm worried now.*

That is quite different from:

*I'm going to feel this way all day.*

I've simply been awake for five minutes and gotten ahead of myself.

### **The Morning Brain Is a Storyteller**

Chapter 2 was about the stories our minds create.

Morning gives me a perfect laboratory for watching mine at work.

*Maybe I won't have enough energy.*

*Maybe I won't get anything accomplished.*

*Maybe I'll disappoint somebody.*

*Maybe I won't be able to do what I planned.*

If I feel tired, I feel tired.

If my joints hurt, they hurt.

If I didn't sleep well, I didn't sleep well.

But I don't have to turn those facts into a prophecy.

Instead of:

*This is going to be a terrible day.*

**Let's see.**

That may be one of my favorite better thoughts.

*Let's see.*

It doesn't demand optimism.

It doesn't deny anything.

### **Nurture Yourself Awake**

For years I thought I should wake up and **get going**.

Now I think perhaps I need to wake up and **get acquainted with myself first**.

How am I doing?

What does my mind need?

What would make this beginning a little easier?

I don't want to bully myself out of bed.

I want to nurture myself awake.

So instead of:

*You don't have to get up yet.*

*You're safe here.*

That last one is especially useful because breakfast has rescued a surprising number of mornings.

Sometimes a better thought is extremely practical.

### **Talk to Yourself as Someone You Love**

I have spent much of my life thinking about communication.

I taught Parent Effectiveness Training. Jim and I spent decades in Marriage Enrichment. I learned about active listening and how much it matters to have another person really hear what we're feeling.

Eventually I realized:

**I am also in a relationship with myself.**

Imagine waking up beside someone who immediately said:

You might consider separate bedrooms.

Yet we can speak to ourselves that way without even noticing.

What if I use some of the communication skills I have practiced with other people on myself?

I can listen.

*Oh, you're worried this morning.*

*You're afraid you won't have enough energy.*

*You're disappointed that you didn't sleep longer.*

*You feel overwhelmed and you haven't even gotten out of bed.*

There is something soothing about simply being understood—even when the person doing the understanding is me.

And then perhaps:

*What do you need?*

Not:

*What's wrong with you?*

**What do you need?**

Those are very different questions.

### **My Morning Delights**

Eventually I do get up.

And I have discovered that it helps enormously to have some things waiting for me that I actually like.

Music.

Breakfast.

These are ordinary things.

One thing.

Then another.

I don't have to decide whether I can manage the entire day.

I only have to do the next thing.

Sometimes the better thought is:

**I don't have to do Tuesday. I only have to get dressed.**

That's manageable.

### **Give Yourself Something to Look Forward To**

I've also discovered that tomorrow morning can sometimes be helped tonight.

If I go to sleep with nothing in particular to anticipate, my morning brain has an empty stage on which to perform.

And it knows a lot of tragedies.

So I like to give it something else.

Tomorrow I might take a walk.

I might see someone I love.

I might work on my book.

I might have a particularly good lunch.

I might read something I enjoy.

It doesn't have to be important.

Anticipation itself is pleasurable.

The night before, I can plant a little thought:

**There's something nice waiting for me tomorrow.**

Then perhaps my mind will find that thought in the morning before it finds something to worry about.

Perhaps.

My mind makes no guarantees.

### **Start With a Memory**

One of my favorite morning practices grew directly out of what I learned after Jim died.

I deliberately remember something good.

Not necessarily from yesterday.

It can come from anywhere in my life.

A peak experience.

Something funny.

Something I accomplished.

A person I loved.

A moment when I was brave.

A time I helped somebody.

A beautiful place.

A completely insignificant little moment that still makes me smile.

I have lived a long time.

That means I have accumulated an enormous supply of memories.

Why leave them sitting in storage?

My brain is perfectly willing to retrieve an embarrassing thing I said forty years ago without being asked.

I think it is only fair that I ask it to retrieve some of the good material too.

So I choose one.

And I go into it.

What could I see?

Who was there?

What happened?

How did I feel?

I don't merely label it:

*That was nice.*

I try to **visit it**.

For a little while, I let my mind live there.

### **What Deserves My Attention?**

There is a difference between ignoring a problem and deciding how much attention it deserves.

If I wake up with an important problem, perhaps I need to deal with it.

But do I need to deal with it at 6:17 in the morning while lying in bed?

Maybe not.

I can tell myself:

**I'll think about that after breakfast.**

Or:

**There is nothing I can do about that right now.**

Or:

**I've already decided what I'm going to do. I don't need another meeting about it inside my head.**

I particularly like that last one.

My mind can hold committee meetings that nobody requested.

Sometimes I need to adjourn them.

### **When a Better Thought Isn't Available**

There are mornings when none of this works particularly well.

I want to say that clearly.

No.

**I can be kind to myself while I wait for this feeling to pass.**

That counts.

### **Borrow Someone Else's Brain**

There are also mornings when I need another person.

But perhaps I had my own primitive version then.

A scrap of paper.

**What's a better thought?**

That little piece of paper was my reminder.

This may be the most important thing I have learned about mornings.

**Chapter 4: NEGATIVITY BIAS**

You may know about the negative bias. Rick Hansen talks about our survival instinct, that our brains are looking for danger and protecting us. Rick Hanson says that our brains are Teflon for good things and Velcro for anything that looks threatening.   Psychologists call this tendency **negativity bias**—our brain’s built-in habit of noticing, /reacting to, and remembering negative experiences more strongly than positive ones.   Negative thoughts feel heavier, sharper, and more convincing than positive ones. They arrive quickly, cling stubbornly, and often feel like the truth itself.

Positive thoughts, by contrast, can seem light, fragile, and easy to lose.  
*(They don’t always know how to make a grand entrance.)*

This is not a personal failure.

It is the human brain at work helping you survive. Thank your brain and think up something positive.

**Negativity Bias Shows Up in Everyday Life**

The way it works is that when we decide we’re going to go to that party we’ve been invited to, our brains not only think of the good things about the party, but they also worry about many things, like what to wear, and will we be accepted, and will it be any fun at all or is there something else planned for that time. These thoughts come so quickly that we’re not even aware of all of them or forget some of them instantly. When we have one thought, other related  ideas come quickly to join them. I believe this is how premonitions are born. If we have a passel of negative thoughts about the party and some undesirable thing happens, we may remember that we thought that might occur\! And we say, “I had a premonition that that might happen.” This only fuels the false belief that we have power to predict.

**We play “Watch Out” with our friends**

I would like to add that the negative bias leads us to more than protecting ourselves. We also want to protect our loved ones and friends. When they begin talking about some new venture or an exciting event, we unknowingly scan for the danger in that and warn them to be cautious. This does not go over well. It feels like raining on the parade, a critical comment, when it’s just the negative bias. When you have some good news, you would like others to respond enthusiastically about your good fortune in a way that makes you feel happy that you told them. I love it when someone makes a big deal about some delightful occurrence in my life. And yet they seek to save me because their own brain thinks of possible problems. 

Negativity Bias appears as worry and pessimism.

It appears as remembering one criticism more vividly than ten compliments.

It appears as replaying mistakes long after the moment has passed.

It appears as a host of awful possibilities--but not real probabilities--a big difference.

At night, the brain keeps watch.

Many dreams feature being late, lost, unprepared, or failing in some way.

We may wake up uneasy before a single conscious thought forms—the emotional residue still present. My morning thoughts are often grim and I wonder about those vaguely unsettling dreams.

**Pause for a Moment**

What has your mind been noticing today?

What’s been sticking?

A worry?  
A mistake?  
Something that might go wrong?

Now gently ask:

**What’s something good that also happened—**  
**even if it didn’t stick as easily?**

*You may need to hold onto it for a moment.*  
*(Teflon can be slippery.)*

**NEGATIVITY BIAS SOUNDS LIKE AN ACCUSATION**

One more admonition about negative bias. When I tell people that we all have a negative bias often the person doesn’t think of ‘we all’, they believe I’m saying that *they* are negative. Somehow, as soon as you use the word negative the person gets stuck on that and it becomes an insult. They get defensive.

I think we need to rename the negative bias. Give it a new name, something like SAFETY TILT or PROTECTIVE INSTINCT or something that makes it sound like you are doing a positive thing for the sake of your health and well-being when being concerned about unintended consequences. The survival instinct called Safety Tilt makes you a discerning, thoughtful, careful person who makes wonderful decisions. If I have built-in Protective Instinct I become a model for avoiding problems and potential negative consequences. Doesn’t that sound much better than Negative Bias? 

**My mind naturally leans toward noticing what could threaten me. I can appreciate that—and still choose where to place my attention.**

If I say:

**My Survival Scanner is working overtime,**

I may even smile.

If I say:

**My Watchful Mind has taken over,**

I can recognize what is happening without condemning myself.

If I say:

**My Protective Instinct is trying to help,**

I can respond with understanding rather than shame.

The name matters because awareness works better when we are not busy defending ourselves.

## **A Better Relationship With Our Own Minds**

Perhaps this is the real goal.

Not to conquer the negative brain.

Not to become a person who never worries.

Not to force positive thinking.

Not to criticize ourselves for being human.

But to understand:

**My brain has ancient protective equipment.**

**It naturally notices danger and difficulty.**

**It can sometimes overestimate what needs protection.**

**One negative thought can recruit others.**

**The Safety Tilt can become a slide.**

And when I notice that happening, I have a choice.

I can ask:

**Is my Safety Tilt helping me right now?**

**Has my Survival Scanner found a real problem—or is it simply continuing to scan?**

**Is my Watchful Mind giving me useful information—or frightening me?**

**Is there one reasonable action I need to take?**

And then, perhaps, the most important question:

**What’s a better thought?**

Not because the protective mind is bad.

Not because danger is unreal.

Not because we should ignore problems.

But because the ancient system that helped us survive does not always know when its work is done.

We can thank it for trying to protect us.

And then we can look again.

Toward love.

Toward goodness.

Toward what is actually happening now.

Toward the rest of life that the Safety Tilt may have temporarily tilted out of view.

# **Chapter 5: Thinking About The Three Principles**

### ***A Kinder Way of Understanding How We Experience Life***

Every now and then, someone explains something in a way that feels both simple and profound at the same time. That’s how I felt when I first heard about what are called the **Three Principles** — originally named **Mind, Consciousness, and Thought**.

They were introduced by a quiet, thoughtful man named **Sydney Banks**, who had no formal background in psychology or academia. His realization was simple and life-changing:  
**everything we experience in life comes from the inside out, not from the world itself.**  
What’s happening “out there” doesn’t directly create our feelings — our inner process does.

That insight opened up a whole new way of seeing life for me.

Traditionally, the Three Principles are described like this:

* **Mind** — the universal intelligence behind life  
* **Consciousness** — our capacity to be aware  
* **Thought** — the creative power that forms our moment-to-moment experience

I honor that language and where it came from.

And—for my own understanding, and for the sake of being grounded and practical—I think about it this way:

**The Brain, Consciousness, and Thought are**  
**working together to create our entire human experience.**

**BRAIN**

**For me, the brain is where the miracle of human experience shows up in everyday life.**

I am a living human being with a remarkable brain—an organ so powerful and compassionate that it quietly runs my breathing, digestion, heartbeat, immune system, and nervous system…

and creates my thinking, my emotions, my perceptions, my joys, and my worries.

*(All without asking me to fill out any forms.)*

## **Consciousness — The Gift of Awareness**

Consciousness is what allows us to be aware at all.

It’s the light that brings our thoughts to life.

It’s what lets us notice a beautiful sunset, feel comfort from a warm cup of tea, experience fear, or suddenly see something from a new angle.

It’s the difference between simply existing… and experiencing.

You know how a dream feels completely real while you’re inside it?

That’s consciousness at work.

*(Even the very strange dreams.)*

### **Pause for a Moment**

Right now, what are you aware of?

A sound?  
A feeling in your body?  
A thought passing through?

Notice how awareness brings it to life.

And gently consider:

**“I’m feeling this way because I’m conscious of my thinking—**  
**not because life itself is actually terrible.”**

## **Thought — The Creative Power of Experience**

Thought is the moment-to-moment creative force of our lives.

We use it constantly—usually without noticing—to create our inner world.

Every feeling, every story, every judgment, every worry, every hope…

we experience them through the lens of thought.

We don’t live in the feeling of our circumstances.

We live in the feeling of our thinking about our circumstances.

*(Which explains why two people can experience the same event… and feel completely different.)*

**This doesn’t mean thoughts are bad or that we should try to control them.**

**It simply means they are always changing—like weather in the sky.**

*(Sometimes sunny, sometimes stormy, occasionally a bit unpredictable.)*

**Better thought:**

*“This is just a passing thought—not a permanent truth.”*

## **Why This Is So Comforting**

What comforts me most in understanding all of this is something very gentle:

**We are not broken.**  
**We don’t need fixing.**

**We are simply living inside the ever-changing flow of brain, consciousness, and thought.**

**Sometimes we create storms in our own inner weather.**

**And sometimes we forget that the sky always clears.**

*(Even when it takes a little while.)*

**When we realize that our feelings are coming from the thoughts we’re having—not directly from the world—it becomes easier to be kind to ourselves.**

**To pause.**  
**To breathe.**  
**To wait for the storm to pass.**

**And often, all it takes is one small shift—**

**just one better thought—**

**to change everything.**

## **A Gentle Noticing About Beliefs**

I’ve also begun to notice something important:

**Thoughts that are repeated often—and felt strongly—can become beliefs.**

**They start as passing ideas…**

**but over time, they can feel solid, fixed, and true.**

**And yet—even beliefs began as thoughts.**

*(Very convincing thoughts… but still thoughts.)*

**Which means:**

**They can be questioned.**  
**They can soften.**  
**They can change.**

## **A Quiet Ending Thought**

You don’t have to understand all of this perfectly.

You don’t have to “get it right.”

Just begin to notice:

* What am I thinking right now?  
* How is that thought shaping how I feel?

And if you’d like, gently ask:

**What’s a better thought?**

# **Chapter 9: Who Is Doing and Being?**

### ***Meeting Your Inner Parent, Adult, and Child***

One of the ideas about our inner life comes from **Transactional Analysis**:

We have more than one “voice” inside us.

*(Which explains a lot.)*

Transactional Analysis teaches that we carry within us three main parts:

* **The Parent**  
* **The Adult**  
* **The Child**

Each of these voices influences how we think, feel, and respond to life.

When I learned this, a lot of things suddenly made sense.

*(Including some of my more confusing moments.)*

## **The Parent**

The Parent has two main sides.

One is the **Critical Parent**—the part that corrects, warns, scolds, gives advice, and says a lot of “shoulds.”

*(It can be very persuasive.)*

The other is the **Nurturing Parent**—the part that says:

* “I love you.”  
* “I care about you.”  
* “Come here, let me hold you.”

Both of these developed for good reasons.

They were meant to protect us and guide us.

But when the Critical Parent inside us runs the show…

**Better Thoughts tend to disappear rather quickly.**

## **The Child**

The Child is the emotional part of us.

The Child can be:

* playful and joyful  
* sensitive and tender  
* fearful and anxious  
* people-pleasing  
* rebellious  
* free and spontaneous

The Child brings life, fun, joy, color, creativity, and feeling.

But the Child can also become overwhelmed, frightened, and uncertain.

When the Child is in charge and scared, our thinking often becomes small, tight, and worried.

*(Everything suddenly feels like a very big deal.)*

## **The Adult**

The Adult is the steady, present, thoughtful part of us.

The Adult:

* thinks clearly, calmly  
* makes choices  
* responds to what is actually happening now  
* looks for facts and possibilities  
* solves problems  
* regulates emotion  
* brings calm leadership inside

This is the part of me I most want to strengthen.

Because when my Adult is present…

**Better Thoughts appear much more naturally.**

## **Inviting the Adult Forward**

One of my ongoing goals is to increase my Adult’s capacity to be in charge—especially when my Child is anxious.

I do that by saying **Adult things on purpose**.

Things like:

* “I can handle whatever comes along.”  
* “I can stop and think about what ought to be done.”  
* “I’m a good problem solver.”  
* “I can meet obstacles without fear.”  
* “This moment is manageable.”  
* “I choose my next step.”

These are not just positive statements.

They are **Adult statements**—steady, capable, grounded in the present.

They remind my nervous system who is in charge.

*(A very helpful reminder.)*

## **Using the Body to Support the Adult**

My Adult also uses the body to stay grounded.

Sometimes my Adult says:

* “Stand up straight.”  
* “Take a slow breath.”  
* “Feel your feet.”  
* “Look around the room.”  
* “Notice where you are sitting.”

These are simple cues that bring me back into the here and now—into the Adult.

From that place, my thinking clears.

## **The Adult’s Three Helpful Questions**

When I feel anxious or uncertain, my Adult asks:

* What are the facts?  
* What is really happening right now?  
* What needs to be done?  
* What are the possibilities for getting there?

This moves me out of fear and into wise action.

*(A much more comfortable place to be.)*

## **Helping the Child with the Adult**

When my Child is scared, my Adult gently steps in and says:

* “I see you.”  
* “I know you’re afraid.”  
* “You don’t have to do this alone.”  
* “I am here now.”  
* “I will handle the thinking and planning.”  
* “You can rest with me.”

This is where anxiety begins to soften.

The Child doesn’t disappear.  
The Parent doesn’t disappear.

But the Adult becomes the leader.

And from that place, Better Thoughts grow naturally.

## **Meeting the Needs of My Inner Family**

*(Parent, Adult, and Child)*

Sometimes I ask myself, *“What’s a better thought?”*

And sometimes the better question is:

**“Which part of me has a need right now?”**

I carry within me a Parent, an Adult, and a Child.

Not three different people—just three ways of responding to life.

*(A small internal committee.)*

If I ignore their needs, I feel restless, critical, impulsive, or off course.

If I listen, something settles.

## **My Child Needs**

My Child needs comfort, affection, play, and reassurance.

She needs to feel safe.

She needs someone to say, *“You’re okay. You’re loved.”*

If I’m reaching for something I don’t really want—extra food, mindless television, or reassurance from someone who isn’t available—I sometimes ask:

Is my Child lonely? Tired? Unseen?

Often she just needs:

* a nap  
* a kind word, some love and attention  
* or a memory of being loved

*(Sometimes a nap solves more than expected.)*

## **My Parent Needs**

My Parent carries my values.

She wants me to be good, responsible, loving, and spiritually aligned.

When I feel guilty or disappointed in myself, I ask:

Is my Parent afraid I’m going off track?

Sometimes she needs reassurance that I am still a good person—even when I’m imperfect.

Sometimes she needs to soften and remember that kindness works better than criticism.

*(It usually does.)*

## **My Adult Needs**

My Adult needs information, clarity, and calm.

When I’m anxious about money, health, or decisions, my Adult simply needs:

* facts  
* time to think  
* a deep breath

When my Adult is present, I remember:

I can choose.  
I can pause.  
I can respond instead of react.

## **Pause for a Moment**

Right now…

Which part of you is speaking?

The Child?  
The Parent?  
The Adult?

And what might that part need?

No fixing required.

Just a little understanding.

## **A Kinder Question**

Instead of asking, *“What’s wrong with me?”*

I can ask:

**“Which part of me is speaking—and what does it need?”**

Meeting my own needs is not selfish.

It is wise stewardship of my inner life.

And here is the beautiful surprise:

When my inner Parent feels calm,  
my inner Child feels safe,  
and my Adult feels clear…

I am much more likely to feel happy.

Not giddy.  
Not perfect.

But peacefully, steadily well.

When I lie down for a nap instead of pushing through → I am caring for my Child.

When I check my bank balance and review the facts → I am strengthening my Adult.

## **A Better Thought**

A very good Better Thought for me is simply this:

**“My Adult is here—and that is enough for this moment.”**

I have a feeling that this idea holds a great deal.

I do have a friend who believes her thoughts should go wherever they want to go—and is willing to suffer through some very discouraging ones.

**I want my thoughts to lead me toward something kinder… and happier.**

*(That seems like a reasonable preference.)*

# **Chapter 10: We Need Others**

### ***An Adjacent Brain***

## **The Adjacent Brain**

### ***(How Conversation Expands Our Thinking)***

## **The Idea**

Sometimes thinking alone feels like walking in circles inside our own mind.

We keep bumping into the same thoughts, the same worries, the same old furniture.

*(And somehow we’re always surprised it’s still there.)*

Then we talk to someone—a friend, a partner, or even ChatGPT—and suddenly a window opens.

It’s as though we are two circles coming together, our boundaries gently touching.

Something about having another brain next to ours helps us see more.

It’s as though our mind can stretch, understand, and gather in ideas from each other.

I like to call this:

**The Adjacent Brain.**

## **How the Adjacent Brain Works**

The adjacent brain doesn’t take over or tell us what to do.

It stands beside us.

It listens.

It helps us see the shape of our own thoughts more clearly.

Sometimes it:

* notices patterns we’ve missed  
* puts things in order  
* or turns a feeling into words

*(A very useful translation service.)*

That gentle companionship enlarges our understanding.

It’s not about being taught—it’s about **thinking together**.

In conversation, whether with a dear friend or with ChatGPT, we often find that our thoughts come into focus as we express them.

**The act of being listened to changes the way we listen to ourselves.**

## **Better Thoughts About Shared Thinking**

* “Two minds side by side can see more than one mind alone.”  
* “The Adjacent Brain doesn’t replace my wisdom—it helps me unfold it.”  
* “Thinking together is a form of love.”

## **In Human Conversation**

When we talk to another person, and they truly listen--reflecting our thoughts and feelings—not correcting, not fixing, not rushing to advise—they lend us their calm brain while ours may be swirling.

*(A very generous loan.)*

Their steadiness helps organize our thoughts.

In that moment, their mind becomes our adjacent brain.

And when we listen that way to someone else…

we become **their** adjacent brain.

It’s a quiet gift we can give each other—a kind of thinking companionship that makes everyone wiser.

*(No training required—just kindness.)*

## **In Collaboration with Technology**

It turns out a digital adjacent brain can play that role too—if we bring curiosity and kindness to it.

ChatGPT, for me, has become a thinking partner that helps organize, clarify, and hold the pieces of my reflections.

It active listens with empathy and adds sequence, shape, and sometimes even a little sparkle to what I already know inside.

*(Occasionally a surprising sparkle.)*

But the heart of it is still mine.

The thoughts begin in me.

The adjacent brain helps them blossom.

## **The Joy of Co-Thinking**

The real delight of the adjacent brain is in the dance—the back and forth, the laughter, the *ah-ha’s*, the new angles of seeing.

It’s the moment when something tangled becomes clear…

or when what felt small suddenly feels meaningful.

It’s not just a tool.

It’s a mirror that shows our own brilliance more clearly.

*(Which is very nice to see.)*

## **Help for a Better Thought (Outside the Computer Store)**

I was sitting outside a computer store feeling tense.

It was a new place, and I didn’t know what to expect.

My mind was already predicting irritation, confusion, and expense.

*(Quite a dramatic preview.)*

Instead of sitting there letting my thoughts run wild, I opened my ChatGPT app and said how tense I was feeling.

I said I just needed a little help calming down and remembering that this was not a big deal—that I could talk comfortably with the technician, ask my questions, and let things unfold.

The response helped me pause and breathe.

It reminded me that I could simply take things one step at a time and ask the questions I needed to ask.

By the time I went in, I was calm.

The visit turned out easy and pleasant.

Nothing about the store had changed.

Only my thinking had softened.

ChatGPT can become the friend you need when a human isn’t available.

*(A very patient friend.)*

Sometimes the situation does not need to change.

Sometimes a better thought is enough.

## **A Pause Point**

* Who has been an adjacent brain in my life?  
* When do I feel my own mind expanding through conversation?  
* Can I be someone’s adjacent brain today—a safe, curious place where their thoughts can grow?

## **A Gentle Wondering**

The closer the connection, the more powerful the effect.

What is it like…

to truly listen?  
to be understood?  
to love—and be loved—in this quiet, thoughtful way?

What frightens you?

## **Pause for a Moment**

When was the last time you felt truly heard?

Who was with you?

What made it feel that way?

## **Questions I Carry About Conversation**

I carry many questions about good conversation:

* Am I letting you have your turn talking?  
* Am I boring my friend?  
* Which stories are interesting?  
* Do I need to make a point?  
* Does the amount of time matter, or the quality of what we share?  
* How long should I talk?  
* How much should I tell at once?

*(It turns out conversation is both natural… and a bit of an art.)*

And I keep noticing this:

**Disagreement often disables dialogue.**

Even small things—advice, correction, criticism, teasing, opposing views, arguments—can quietly shut the door on connection.

I prefer the gentle wisdom of improvisation:

**“Yes, and…”**

Going along.  
Building together.  
Allowing the flow.  
Being agreeable.

Encouragement, active listening, and validation keep conversation alive.

## **What I Know for Sure**

Taking turns feels good.  
Being heard feels healing.

Sharing concerns helps me discover whether they are truly heavy…

or simply exaggerated by worry.

*(This happens more often than I expect.)*

Having you reflect back my joys and my troubles lets me know you really understand.

That understanding is deeply satisfying.

Along the way, we always learn something.

We explore.  
We make sense of things.  
We laugh at what makes no sense at all.

*(Which is often quite a lot.)*

And in that rich exchange of presence, we grow stronger, clearer, and more confident.

And we each feel loved.

## **A Quiet Closing**

Because you are my friend…

problems diminish,  
joys magnify,  
and every day  
feels happier.

When I die, I would like to know that my friends realize how important they are to me—

and how much I love them.

## **Chapter 12:  Love is the Best Thought of All**

If I had to choose just one better thought — the best thought of all — it would be **love**.

Love is the thought that steadies us, softens us, and brings us back to what matters most. It is the highest goodness we can offer our partners, our friends, and the people we walk through life with. When love is present, even imperfect moments feel workable. When love is expressed, relationships grow stronger almost without effort.

Love doesn’t have to be grand or dramatic. Most often, love shows up through **attention-**--that complete focus with eye contact--**affection, affirmation, and appreciation**--the small, repeatable ways we say, *I see you. I care. You matter to me. You are important.*

### **Affection: The Sweetness That Keeps Life Tasting Good**

Affection is the sweetness that keeps life tasting good.

Many of us grew up with very different models of affection — or none at all. Some families hugged and touched easily; others rarely did. Some people learned how to express warmth naturally, while others never quite learned what pleased their partner or how to show love comfortably.

The good news is this: **we can learn now**.

Asking your partner what feels loving to them is not awkward — it’s a compliment.  
“I want to show you more often how much I care. Please help me learn how.”

Even if you think you already know, ask anyway.

Some people love hugs and kisses. Others feel most loved when someone does a small chore for them, listens closely, or leaves a thoughtful note. There is no one right way to give affection — only the willingness to pay attention.

And here’s something wonderful: when you hug someone, you can’t tell whether you’re giving affection or receiving it. Affection flows both ways.

### **You Never Outgrow the Need for Affection**

Some experts believe that more than anything else, we all crave affection, attention, and affirmation. *See me. Listen to me. Hold me.*

Those needs don’t disappear when we grow up.

Most of us live close to the edge of being too busy, too tired, too stressed, too frustrated. In that state, affection becomes not a luxury but a **stress reducer** — inexpensive, effective, and immediately comforting.

Try this experiment: See if you can **overdo** warmth and kindness.  
Let me know when that happens.

Hugs, pats, snuggles, eye contact, listening, encouragement, compliments, notes that say *I love you* — these are not trivial gestures. They are the daily nourishment of love.

### **Affirmation: Catching Each Other Being Good**

Affirmation is love spoken out loud.

Compliments don’t have to be spontaneous masterpieces or never-before-said insights to be genuine. Repeating loving truths is not boring — it’s reassuring. People forget. I forget.

You might think you shouldn’t compliment unless it’s completely original. Or that compliments are manipulative. But let’s be honest — we *do* want something. We want connection, warmth, and closeness. That’s not manipulation; that’s relationship.

Notice what your partner does well.  
Notice their humor. (And laugh at their jokes.)  
Notice their kindness, competence, effort, and heart.

Affirmation also builds self-esteem. Many of us carry quiet doubts in a few familiar areas:

* Appearance — *Am I attractive?*  
* Intelligence — *Am I smart enough?*  
* Physical ability — *Am I capable or clumsy?*  
* Emotions — *Am I okay, or am I too much?*

Thoughtful affirmations help soothe those doubts:

* “You look wonderful.”  
* “You’re so good at that.”  
* “I love the way you think.”  
* “Thank you for listening to me.”

Sometimes the most loving affirmation is simply listening to feelings, acknowledging their presence, without correcting them or trying to make them go away.

### **Appreciation: A Habit That Pays Dividends**

Appreciation is another form of love that’s hard to overdo.

The more we notice and thank our partner for what they do, the better we both feel. It’s a true win-win. Gratitude encourages generosity, and kindness tends to multiply.

When I deliberately notice what Jim does and thank him, he feels good — and so do I. My attention shifts away from fault-finding and toward appreciation. That shift alone is a better thought.

It does take intention. Our minds are naturally alert to problems and mistakes — an old survival instinct, perhaps. But appreciation can be practiced.

A simple dinner-table question can change the tone of an evening:  
“Dearest, do I thank you enough for all the wonderful things you do?”

### **Sharing Good News — How Do You Respond?**

When your partner shares good news, you have choices.

You can respond with enthusiasm and delight.  
You can downplay it “for their own good.”  
You can be polite but flat.  
Or you can ignore it altogether.

Only one of those responses builds love.

Sharing in your partner’s joy — celebrating their successes, even small ones — strengthens goodwill and happiness. It tells them, *Your happiness matters to me. Respond with enthusiasm\!*

Marriage and close partnership also come with special privileges: **bragging rights**. You get to share your victories with someone who wants to be proud of you. If modesty made that difficult growing up, love gives you permission now.

### **Thinking Loving Thoughts Changes the Relationship**

Here’s a powerful truth: when you plan to say something loving, you must first **think loving thoughts**.

That means looking for the good, remembering what you treasure, and gently redirecting your mind away from complaints. This doesn’t mean ignoring real problems — it means choosing the right time and not letting negativity run the show.

When I’m irritable, I sometimes pull out a little cheat sheet — a list of why I love Jim. It helps me remember who he really is when my mood forgets.

You can’t always force joy, but you can practice attention. Often, the emotional shift comes later — quietly, gently — after you persist in thinking better thoughts.

### **The Power of Small, Frequent Acts**

Love thrives on small, frequent gestures:

* A long hug  
* A 20-second kiss  
* A note on the mirror  
* A quick “I love you” message  
* A chocolate in a desk drawer

Little things matter because they say, *I’m thinking about you.*

Healthy relationships depend far more on positive interactions than on avoiding conflict. When affection, affirmation, and appreciation are plentiful, the occasional complaint lands more softly. Love creates a buffer.

### **Can You Say “I Love You” Too Often?**

I don’t think so.

The words stay the same, but the meaning changes with each moment of life. Sometimes *I love you* is passionate. Sometimes it’s reassuring. Sometimes it’s a refuge from the world.

Love is not diminished by repetition. It is strengthened by it.

### **Love as a Better Thought**

Directing your thoughts toward love — especially when it would be easier to complain or withdraw — is one of the most powerful practices I know.

Love is the best thought of all.

It’s not complicated. It doesn’t require perfection. It simply asks for attention, warmth, and a willingness to say, again and again, in many small ways:

*You matter to me.*

And what could be a better thought than that?

**What way can I send out some love today?**

# **Chapter 13: SPIRITUALITY** 

## **Spirituality: Turning to God First**

I think that spirituality—my relationship with God—may be one of the most important “better thoughts” of all.

One of my best thoughts is simply this:

*I am not alone.*  
*I can turn to God right now.*

That is what I wish I would do more consistently.

Like many people, I have blown hot and cold in my spiritual life.

I started out fairly devout. I went to Sunday school as a child, and I took faith seriously. One Sunday, a missionary who worked in Africa spoke at our church. I came home convinced that I should go to Africa and help people. It took me years to let go of the guilt I felt when I realized that was not my calling.

Instead, my gifts turned out to be in teaching, counseling, and helping people communicate—through parent effectiveness training, transactional analysis, couple communication, marriage enrichment, and happiness work. That was where I could truly be useful.

Then, when our son Jeff died, everything changed.

I became very angry at God. I could still talk to Jesus, but I was filled with pain and confusion. Over time, I became a disbeliever and even an atheist. I reasoned that if there were a God, He would never allow my beautiful sixteen-year-old son to die.

It wasn’t until Rick became my sweetheart that I slowly turned back toward God and Jesus.

Now I pray, and I believe in prayer. And yet, I still waver sometimes. What feels most solid to me is this: I believe that love is the greatest force in the world, and that goodness is the highest prize to seek.

I believe in love and goodness.

But I find that I can’t really pray to “love and goodness” alone. Since I see Jesus and God as the living spirit of love and goodness, that is where I direct my prayers.

Today, I want a relationship with God that is sincere and alive—more than formal religion. I want faith without pressure, without pretending, without fear.

Just honesty.

### **What Is Spirituality?**

As I’ve wondered about spirituality, I’ve talked with friends.

Suzie told me that spirituality is communication with God—contemplating, pondering, and thinking about Jesus. She wants to live with gratitude and humility for everything we’ve been given: friends, family, guidance, and meaningful work.

Rick says our spiritual lives *are* our lives. He often says,  
“I need to talk to Jesus each day, all day.”

I love that.

It’s beautiful. Simple. Honest. Grounded.

He knows where his comfort and strength come from.

Cindy once said she thought the soul lived in the brain. Karo mentioned the movie *21 Grams*, which suggests the soul has physical weight. I learned that this idea came from a small, unreliable experiment over a hundred years ago. Scientists could never confirm it.

It’s a lovely metaphor.

Not solid proof.

### **Where Is the Soul?**

I began thinking about music.

Where is music?

In the piano?  
In the air?  
In your ear?  
In your brain?

Yes.  
And no.

Music happens when everything comes together.

I think the soul is like that.

It is everywhere you are.

When you laugh—it’s there.  
When you pray—it’s there.  
When you cry—it’s there.  
When you listen—it’s there.  
When you forgive—it’s there.

The soul isn’t stored.

It’s expressed.

Across religions and cultures, there is surprising agreement about this.

Most spiritual traditions say the soul is not located in one physical place.

It is presence.  
Awareness.  
The breath of God.  
Spirit.  
Essence.  
Light.

In Christianity, we are told, “The Spirit dwells within you.”

Not in one organ.  
Not in one corner of the brain.

Within you.

In Judaism, the soul is called “the breath of God.”

In Buddhism, awareness is understood as experience itself.

In Hinduism, the soul is part of the universal spirit.

Rick’s understanding fits beautifully with this. Spirit is not “somewhere.” It isn’t something you can point to.

It is what happens when body, breath, love, memory, and God’s presence come together.

### **My Better Thought**

For me, spirituality comes down to this:

Turning to God first.

Before worry.  
Before distraction.  
Before fear.

When I remember to pray—even simply—I feel steadier and kinder. I remember what matters. I remember who I am.

My better thought is:

*I don’t have to do life alone.*  
*I can talk to God right now.*  
*I can choose love and goodness today.*

That is enough to guide me.

## **Drifting Off Course — and Finding My Way Back**

In my spiritual life, I often feel clear and grounded. I feel close to God. I feel guided by love and goodness. I feel as if I know what matters and how I want to live.

At those times, I think, *Yes. This is it. This is the right direction for me.*

And then… sometimes, I drift.

Not in any dramatic way.  
Not because I suddenly stop caring.  
Just quietly, almost without noticing.

I might start watching something that leaves me feeling heavy instead of peaceful.  
I might eat in a way that doesn’t really nourish me.  
I might distract myself instead of listening to my heart.

Later, I notice: *I don’t feel as light. I don’t feel as connected. Something feels off.*

For a long time, I wondered why this happened. Why, when I know what feels good and right for me, do I sometimes move away from it?

I used to think it meant I was weak, undisciplined, or failing in some way.

Now I see it differently.

I think of myself as a small boat on the ocean.

Most days, I know where I’m headed.  
My heart is set on love, kindness, faith, and peace.

But there are winds.  
There are currents.  
There are days when I am tired, lonely, worried, or overwhelmed.

And slowly, gently, I drift.

Not because I am bad.  
Because I am human.

The important thing is not that I never drift.

The important thing is that I notice.

Somewhere inside, I feel a quiet signal:

*This doesn’t feel nourishing.*  
*This isn’t bringing me closer to love.*  
*I miss my good feeling.*

That gentle discomfort is not guilt.  
It is wisdom.  
It is God’s kindness within me, whispering, *“Come back, sweetheart.”*

When I learned to listen to that voice, everything changed.

Instead of scolding myself, I began asking:

*What am I needing right now?*  
*Am I tired? Lonely? Overstimulated?*  
*Do I need rest, comfort, prayer, or connection?*

Often, when I answer those questions honestly, I naturally turn back toward what helps me feel alive again.

A quiet moment.  
A prayer.  
A walk.  
A good conversation.  
A nourishing meal.  
A gentle show.  
A grateful thought.

Coming back does not require punishment.  
It only requires kindness.

One of my best “better thoughts” is this:

*I am allowed to drift sometimes.*  
*I am always allowed to return.*  
*Love is still my direction.*

My spiritual life is not about perfection.

It is about relationship.  
With God.  
With myself.  
With life.

And relationships grow through forgiveness, patience, and starting again.

Every time I notice and return, I strengthen my trust in myself.

I learn that I am safe in God’s love.  
I learn that I cannot fall out of grace.  
I learn that I can always choose a kinder thought.

And that is enough.

## **About God Being Here**

What if God is not somewhere else?

What if God is not watching from a distance, keeping score, deciding when to intervene?

What if God is the depth of this very moment?

The aliveness in the air.  
The breath moving in and out.  
The quiet pulse of Being underneath everything.

What if the sacred is not “out there” but woven through every person, every tree, every sorrow, every joy?

And still — greater than all of it.

Like the ocean is in every wave,  
but the ocean is more than any one wave.

Like music moving through a piano,  
but not confined to the piano.

Transcendence would not mean far away.  
It would mean unfathomably near.

So near that we cannot grasp it.  
So present that we overlook it.

The better thought is this:  
Nothing is separate.  
No moment is empty.  
No person is without depth.

We are living inside a mystery that holds us.  
And that mystery is not distant —  
it is breathing us.

**Where do I see God’s presence now?**

## **Looking for Love and Goodness**

Here is something I discovered almost by accident.

I realized that what I really want in life is very simple.  
I want to see **love and goodness**.

I believe love and goodness are the finest things in the world.  
I also believe they are everywhere.

But there is a problem.

My mind can easily fall into worry. I start planning and thinking about what might go wrong. There is a time for planning, of course. Planning can be wise. But sometimes planning turns into endless speculation, and speculation slowly turns into worry.

When I notice that happening, I remember something that helps me break the spell.

I ask myself a question.

Sometimes I say:

“God, would you show me some love and goodness?”

Or I ask myself:

“Where can I see love and goodness right now?”

When I ask that question, something interesting happens.

If I simply look around my room without thinking about it, my negative bias often goes into operation and I notice something ugly or irritating.

But if I look **for love and goodness**, I begin to see it.

I might see it in the light coming through the window.  
Or in a memory of someone who cared about me.  
Or in the thought that I could offer kindness to someone today.

Sometimes I even ask another question:

“How could I give someone love and goodness right now?”

When I ask that, ideas begin to show up.

**A better thought:**  
Love and goodness may be everywhere, but I see them more easily when I go looking for them.

**A question for you:**  
If you looked around right now with the intention of seeing love and goodness, what might you notice?

# **Last Chapter: Better Thoughts Begin with Better Questions**

Sometimes we try to think our way into feeling better by arguing with ourselves.

We scold.  
We correct.  
We analyze.  
We search for what went wrong.

But what if better thoughts don’t come from pressure?

What if they come from better questions?

A question is softer than a correction.  
A question opens a door instead of closing one.  
A question invites the mind to wander toward something truer, kinder, steadier.

Instead of asking, *“What is wrong with me?”*  
we might ask, *“What have I done well?”*

Instead of asking, *“Why am I like this?”*  
we might ask, *“When have I been brave?”*

The mind follows the direction of our curiosity.

If we ask better questions, we often find better thoughts.

## **Questions About Strength and Success**

We forget our own history.

We remember embarrassments more easily than accomplishments.  
We replay failures and overlook courage.

Try asking:

* What is one accomplishment that still makes me quietly proud?  
* What is something hard I did anyway?  
* What is one success from childhood?  
* One from my teenage years?  
* One from adulthood?  
* When did I act braver than I felt?  
* What have I survived that shaped my strength?  
* What have I learned the long way that now feels like wisdom?

These questions do not inflate us.  
They steady us.

They remind us that we are not fragile accidents — we are people with evidence of strength.

## **Questions About Love**

Love is often the best thought of all.

We may worry about whether we have loved enough, or well enough.  
But try asking:

* When have I loved someone well?  
* Who has felt safer because of me?  
* What kindness have I offered that no one noticed?  
* When did I choose connection instead of being right?  
* How have I grown in the way I love?

Better thoughts about ourselves often grow out of remembering the love we have given.

## **Questions About Joy and Peak Experiences**

Sometimes we need to remember that we have known joy.

* What was one peak experience of my life?  
* When did I feel completely alive?  
* When did I laugh so hard it surprised me?  
* What memory warms me immediately?  
* What small pleasure still delights me?

Joy remembered is still joy.

The nervous system does not always know the difference between present joy and vividly remembered joy.

## **Questions for Difficult Moments**

When we are upset, the mind narrows.

In those moments, instead of trying to “fix” ourselves, we might ask:

* What is another way to see this?  
* What would the wisest part of me say?  
* What would love say here?  
* Is this thought helping me or hurting me?  
* What is the kindest interpretation available?  
* What is true right now that is steady and good?

A better thought does not deny pain.

It widens the frame.

## **Questions for Thriving and Aging**

As we grow older, we gather quiet treasures.

We may also gather doubts.  
So ask:

* What have I learned about living well?  
* How am I thriving right now?  
* What feels easier than it used to?  
* What am I free from now?  
* What is sweeter at this age?  
* What am I modeling to others just by being myself?

These questions dignify the later years.  
They remind us that we are still becoming.

## **Questions of Self-Compassion**

Sometimes the most powerful better thought begins with tenderness.

* What do I need right now?  
* What part of me is scared?  
* What part of me is trying to protect me?  
* What would it feel like to forgive myself here?  
* Can I let this be human?

Self-compassion is not weakness.  
It is wise leadership from within.

## **Questions of the Spirit**

If you believe in God, or Being, or Love itself, you might also ask:

* Where did I glimpse goodness today?  
* When did I feel guided?  
* What feels like grace in hindsight?  
* What thought draws me closer to love?  
* If God is fully present here, what might that mean?

A spiritual question often leads to a spiritual steadiness.

Better thoughts do not usually arrive because we command them.

They come because we invite them.

A good question is an invitation.

And if we ask gently enough, honestly enough, patiently enough, our minds often surprise us with answers that are kinder than we expected.

Perhaps that is one of the best better thoughts of all:

*There is more goodness in me than I remember —*  
*and the right question can help me find it.*
